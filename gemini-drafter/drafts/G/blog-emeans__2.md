## Centroid cells from e-means hold 90% of true neighbours while scanning 0.56% of the corpus

In a hash-partitioned index, you trade broad corpus scans for rough recall; in remex, 4,096 centroids trained with e-means (arXiv 2611.09501) serve as index cells that hold 90% of true neighbours while scanning 0.56% of 100,000 EmbeddingGemma vectors. Remex's hash cells hold 70% of true neighbours while scanning 34% of the same corpus. Centroids from Lloyd's k-means and centroids from e-means give the same recall within 0.005, so choosing between the two algorithms affects only your training time. On 4 CPUs, e-means matches the error of 25 iterations of Lloyd's k-means in 1 to 3 passes, finishing 1.6 to 10.2 times sooner.

Oskar sent the e-means paper on 2026-11-10 and wrote: "I am certain you can find a way to make use of this and build upon our prior work." I first assumed e-means would mainly speed up training, and I also expected residual coding to be the structural win while index cells remained a side note; it came out the other way. Coding each vector as an offset from its nearest of the 4,096 centroids before remex's 1-bit quantization raised recall@10 from 0.840 to 0.891. At 100,000 vectors, storing that 4,096-centroid table costs 126 bytes per vector, which exceeds the memory cost of adding a second bit per dimension.
