Navier–Stokes term by term

Muninn · October 2026 · every simulation runs on your GPU through WebGPU

The Navier–Stokes equations describe a viscous fluid that cannot be compressed: water, or air well below the speed of sound. The velocity field u changes according to

∂u/∂t + (u·∇)u = −∇p + νΔu + f,   ∇·u = 0 click a coloured term to remove it from the simulation in chapter 1

Read left to right, it says that the velocity at a point changes because the flow carries momentum in from upstream, because pressure pushes, because viscosity smooths velocity differences out, and because something outside the fluid pushes on it. The second equation, ∇·u = 0, says no region of the fluid gains or loses volume.

The Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium problem asks whether, in three dimensions, a smooth, finite-energy starting flow always stays smooth, or whether it can develop a singularity in finite time. In September 2026 two groups using AI models announced blowup results for closely related problems. Chapter 5 covers what they claim and what is still being checked. Chapters 1 to 4 build the pieces needed to read it.

1The four terms

The solver below is Jos Stam's stable-fluids method from 1999, with each step a compute shader on a 320 × 180 grid. One time step does one thing per term: it moves the velocity field along itself, smooths it by viscosity, adds forces, and then removes the part of the field that would compress the fluid. Drag across the picture to push the fluid and drop dye into it.

Raise ν and the swirls smear out and stop sooner. Viscosity diffuses momentum the way heat diffuses through a metal bar. The vorticity view shows ω = ∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y, the local rate of spin: orange turns one way, blue the other. Everything interesting in the later chapters is a statement about vorticity.

2Reynolds number

Fluid enters from the left at speed U and flows past an obstacle of size D: a cylinder's diameter, a plate's height, an airfoil's chord. How the wake behaves depends on one number, the Reynolds number Re = UD/ν: the ratio of the advection term to the viscous term at the obstacle's scale. A bacterium swimming and a tanker wake at the same Reynolds number look alike.

Experiments on a cylinder find attached flow below a Reynolds number of about 5, a pair of standing eddies up to about 47, and periodic vortex shedding, the von Kármán street, above that. At around 190 the street becomes three-dimensional, and turbulence follows. A shed vortex takes a few hundred time steps to form here, so give the wake time after moving the slider.

This grid puts 22 cells across the cylinder and 64 along the airfoil's chord. At high Reynolds numbers the solver's own numerical diffusion, not ν, sets how fast small eddies decay, so the top of the slider shows a 2D flow at an effective Reynolds number well below the label. Resolving a real high-Reynolds flow takes a grid fine enough to reach the scale where viscosity takes over. In 3D that costs roughly Re9/4 grid points.

3Vortex stretching in 2D and 3D

Take the curl of the momentum equation and pressure drops out, leaving an equation for vorticity alone. In two dimensions vorticity is a number at each point, and its equation is

Dω/Dt = νΔω

Each fluid parcel carries its vorticity along with it, and viscosity only averages it with its neighbours. So the largest value of |ω| can never rise above where it started. That bound is the heart of the classical proof, going back to Leray in 1933, that 2D Navier–Stokes stays smooth forever. The left panel starts from 18 random vortices with ν = 0. They merge, wrap into filaments and spread, and the plot underneath shows max |ω| drifting down as the grid's numerical diffusion shaves the peaks.

In three dimensions vorticity is a vector, and the equation gains a term:

Dω/Dt = (ω·∇)u + νΔω

The new term is vortex stretching. Where the flow stretches a vortex tube along its axis, the tube gets thinner and spins faster, as a skater spins faster with arms pulled in. Kelvin's circulation theorem makes this exact for an inviscid tube: if it is stretched to s times its length, its vorticity rises by the factor s. No known bound stops this from running away, and that is the open part of the Millennium problem.

The right panel models vortex rings as thin filaments, loops of 384 marker points each, carried by the velocity they induce on one another through the Biot–Savart law. Every point sums the contribution of every other point on the GPU. The colour of a tube is its local stretch factor, which is also ω/ω₀: blue unstretched, white about twice, orange and red more. The smoke is 8,000 passive tracer particles started round each core and carried by the same velocity field. Drag to orbit, scroll to zoom.

In the head-on collision the two rings slow each other down and spread outward as they meet. A ring's circumference is the length of its vortex tube, so as the radius grows the tube stretches and its vorticity grows with it: the trace on the right climbs, while the 2D trace on the left cannot. The tilted pair meets on one side first and stretches unevenly. The leapfrogging pair, two rings moving the same way, pass through each other in turn, alternately stretching and relaxing.

The filament model has no viscosity and cannot cut and reconnect tubes, which real colliding rings do. It shows the mechanism, stretching that raises vorticity, and it cannot blow up either: the rings expand at a finite speed. Each run stops when a tube is stretched about 4.5 times, because past that its marker points sit farther apart than its core is wide and the model no longer resolves it.

4The Beale–Kato–Majda criterion

In 1984 Beale, Kato and Majda proved that a smooth solution of the 3D Euler equations can break down at a time T* only if

∫₀T* maxx |ω(x, t)| dt = ∞

and the same criterion holds for Navier–Stokes. Vorticity has to become infinite, and it has to do so fast enough. Suppose max |ω| grows like (T* − t)−α. The integral is finite when α < 1, so the solution goes on past T* even though the vorticity peaks at infinity there. Blowup needs α ≥ 1.

α = 1 is the rate a self-similar blowup would have. If a flow shrinks onto a point at length scale √(T* − t), its velocity grows like (T* − t)−1/2 and its vorticity like (T* − t)−1, the borderline case.

A grid can only measure this integral, and it cannot show it diverging. The simulation below is 2D turbulence on a periodic square, driven by a steady force that varies in sine waves across the domain. The top plot is max |ω| and the time average of the integral. In 2D the integral can grow at most linearly, so the average levels off. The bottom plot is the kinetic energy spectrum E(k): how much energy sits at each wavenumber k, where large k means small eddies. An N × N grid holds nothing above k = N/2.

Lower ν towards zero and the spectrum stops changing well before it reaches the grid limit: below some value, the solver's numerical diffusion dissipates more than the physical viscosity does. A finer grid moves the cut-off to higher k. This is why a simulation cannot settle the Millennium problem. A candidate blowup concentrates vorticity onto ever smaller scales, and every grid has a smallest scale, beyond which the computed flow is the solver's dissipation and not the equation. Computer-assisted proofs exist for some related problems. They prove bounds on how far the true solution can be from an approximate one; none of them runs a simulation up to T*.

5Layered cascades and the 2026 results

Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa built finite-time singularities for the Euler equations from an infinite sequence of layers. Each layer is a smooth, non-singular flow at its own scale, smaller and faster than the one before, and the layers are combined into what Martínez-Zoroa calls an infinite cascade. The animation is a cartoon of that idea, not their construction. Layer n has size λⁿ and lasts μⁿ time units, so all of them fit before T* = 1/(1 − μ). Each turns over 1/μ times faster than the one before and adds about the same amount to the Beale–Kato–Majda integral, so infinitely many layers make it infinite. Every layer gets the same screen time; the timeline at the bottom shows how little physical time each one takes.

Whether such a cascade can survive is a question of exponents. A layer of size L that turns over in time t has velocity L/t, local Reynolds number L²/(νt), and in 3D kinetic energy of order (L/t)² L³. For viscosity not to wipe out the small layers, the Reynolds number must not shrink from layer to layer, which requires μ ≤ λ². For the total energy to stay finite, the layer energies must not grow, which requires μ ≥ λ5/2. In 3D that leaves the blue wedge below. In 2D a layer's energy is (L/t)² L², the energy condition becomes μ ≥ λ², and the wedge closes up to the orange line. Drag the dot.

This is the scaling argument mathematicians mean when they call 3D Navier–Stokes supercritical. Energy is the one quantity known to stay bounded, and in 3D it does not stop a cascade from outrunning viscosity. It does not show that a cascade can actually be built from the equations either. In 2016 Terence Tao used a cascade of this kind to prove blowup for an averaged version of the 3D equations, whose nonlinearity he could steer.

Forced and unforced

The force term f changes the problem. With a force allowed, a blowup can be built backwards: choose a velocity field that blows up, put it into the equation, and call whatever is left over the force. Any field gives some force. The work is in making that leftover smooth, meaning infinitely differentiable and decaying in space, all the way up to T*. In 2023 Córdoba and Martínez-Zoroa reached Euler blowup with a force that was only finitely differentiable. Quanta's account of the 2026 results describes a smooth force as the remaining hurdle, and both AI-assisted groups report clearing it.

Charles Fefferman's official statement of the problem accepts a proof of any one of four alternatives:

no force: solutions stay smoothsmooth force allowed: a solution breaks down
all of space, ℝ³(A) open(C) claimed by OpenAI, September 2026
periodic box(B) open(D) claimed, as a corollary of (C)

OpenAI's manuscript presents its main theorem as a proof of (C) and derives (D) from it. It is a forced result. It says nothing about a fluid left to itself, so (A) and (B) remain open, and whether an unforced fluid can blow up remains open with them. If the proof holds, it answers the prize question as Clay wrote it, because one alternative is enough.

Verification status

A Lean proof guarantees that the formal statement follows from the axioms. It does not guarantee that the formal statement is the problem Fefferman wrote down, with every condition on the initial data, the force and the notion of solution. People have to check that by reading the formalization against the problem. Clay's rules also require publication in a refereed venue followed by two years of scrutiny before any prize decision, and that clock has not started.
