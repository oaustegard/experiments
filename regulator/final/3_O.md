*Interview with Madame Odile Monnier, in the dining room. Third interview of the night.*

MATTHEY: Madame Monnier, I am sorry to keep you up. I know the sergeant has already asked you some of these questions.

MME MONNIER: He has asked me everything, Inspector, and I have answered everything. I do not think I have anything left that is not already in his book. But I suppose you will want it in yours.

MATTHEY: I would like to begin with the evening, if you are able. Not the end of it yet.

MME MONNIER: The evening was the same as every year. My husband gave the dinner and he gave the guests their lesson.

MATTHEY: Their lesson?

MME MONNIER: When the temple struck nine he made everyone take out their watch and hold it up to him. He has done it since we were married. Nobody is allowed to be wrong at his table, and nobody is allowed to be right without being told so. Félix's watch showed twenty minutes to nine. Aurèle scolded him for it.

MATTHEY: Did your husband say anything more about the watch?

MME MONNIER: He said a watch that old should not be carried by a man who will not have it cleaned. That was all. It was not kind, but it was not unusual.

MATTHEY: And after dinner?

MME MONNIER: The doctor and I played piquet in the salon. Casimir plays badly, and he cheats only when he is losing, which is most of the time. We played without a break. The temple struck ten while we were playing, and we went on after that.

MATTHEY: Did he leave the table at any point?

MME MONNIER: He left the cards when he went out to fetch his cigars. He had run out. I did not look at the clock when he went, Inspector. I have told the sergeant so. I never look at clocks when I am playing with Casimir. It is the only way I have ever won.

MATTHEY: As he went out, did he say anything to you?

MME MONNIER: He asked me to play for him.

MATTHEY: And did you?

MME MONNIER: I sat down at the piano at once and played Chopin, which is what I always play when I am asked for music. My husband used to say it was the only thing I did that was worth the price of the piano. I played until he came back, and I stopped when he came in.

MATTHEY: Do you remember when the hall clock struck?

MME MONNIER: A moment after he came back, the hall clock struck eleven.

MATTHEY: Thank you. Now I must ask you about the quarrel at dinner.

MME MONNIER: Between my husband and Monsieur Girard? They quarrelled about the price of the repeater. Aurèle would not take less and Monsieur Girard would not pay more. It was not the first time they had quarrelled over that watch, and I do not suppose it was the last.

MATTHEY: Was there bad feeling between them?

MME MONNIER: There was bad feeling between my husband and most men who wanted something from him. Monsieur Girard simply said so more loudly.

MATTHEY: And Félix?

MME MONNIER: Félix owes money. Some of it, I believe, is owed to Monsieur Girard. I told my husband so last spring. He told me it was not my concern, and that Félix would learn what debt costs when he was ready to learn it.

MATTHEY: Had your husband been generous with Félix in the past?

MME MONNIER: He was generous with Félix's father, when Jules was alive. He was less generous with Jules's son. You may write that down.

MATTHEY: Casimir Vauthier has been a friend of your family for a long time.

MME MONNIER: For thirty years. He sat with Jules in his last illness, and he has sat at our table ever since. He is the oldest friend either of us has, which is not the same as saying he is the closest.

MATTHEY: Madame, you have been married thirty years.

MME MONNIER: Thirty-one, in the spring. We were fond of each other, Inspector. That is not the same as being in love, and at our age I think it is better. It does not wear through so quickly.

MATTHEY: I am sorry to ask this, but how would you describe your feelings for him now?

MME MONNIER: You want me to weep for him in front of a policeman. I will not do it. I will answer your questions, and I will answer them correctly, and that is all I have to give you tonight.
