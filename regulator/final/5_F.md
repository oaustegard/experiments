*Interview with Monsieur Félix Monnier, nephew of the deceased, in the dining room. Fifth interview of the night.*

MATTHEY: Monsieur Monnier, thank you for waiting. I am sorry to keep you up. Your uncle, Monsieur Aurèle Monnier, was found dead in his atelier. You are his nephew?

FÉLIX: His brother's son, yes. My father was Jules. He died in 1889, you will have that in your book already. Uncle Aurèle brought me into the business after that, or he tried to. I suppose I am his heir, and you are going to ask me that, and so will everyone else in town.

MATTHEY: I will ask you about the evening first. Take your time, and tell me in your own words.

FÉLIX: Uncle has done the same thing every year since I was a boy. When the temple bell struck nine he made us all put our watches on the cloth, and then he told each man what was wrong with his. Very entertaining, if you are not the one being told. When the temple bell struck nine, my watch showed twenty minutes to nine. He made a scene about it in front of everyone. Said a man who could not keep his own father's watch going had no business in his house. He was laughing when he said it. Not enough, I thought.

MATTHEY: Your watch. Is it the silver one?

FÉLIX: My father's silver watch. It has been behind for ten years. I have never let anyone regulate it, not a watchmaker, not even Uncle, and he is the best hand in the canton. He asked to see inside it at dinner. I said no. Everyone laughed at that too.

MATTHEY: And after dinner?

FÉLIX: I went up to the library. I had brandy, I will admit that, more than I should have. I stayed there alone. I did not want to talk about money, which is what everyone wanted to talk about.

MATTHEY: Did you hear anything from the library?

FÉLIX: I did. At a quarter past ten by my watch, someone went quickly up the stairs past the library door. I did not see who. The door was shut.

MATTHEY: Go on.

FÉLIX: At eighteen minutes past ten by my watch I heard a thump on the floor right above me, in the atelier. Then my uncle's repeater sounding. Ten slow deep notes, I counted those. Then some doubled notes, and then quick high ones, which I did not count. I know that sound, Inspector. He played it for anyone who would sit still. I thought he had dropped something, or that he was showing somebody the piece. I stayed in the library.

MATTHEY: And after that?

FÉLIX: At twenty-one minutes past ten by my watch, someone came quickly down. Again I did not see who. The door was shut.

MATTHEY: You do not know who it was.

FÉLIX: I do not know, and I would rather not guess aloud. I am the one in this house who was alone directly below the atelier, and I can count as well as anybody what that will look like. You need not say it. I can hear it already.

MATTHEY: I am only asking what you saw and heard. Who came to the library after that?

FÉLIX: Bastien Girard. He came in at thirty-two minutes past ten by my watch, and we sat together there until the temple struck eleven. He wanted to buy the rights to the repeater. Uncle would not take his price, and they quarrelled about it at dinner. I owe Girard money too, some of it. So you see, I was not pleased to have him sitting in my library, and I was less pleased to have him talk about the repeater as if it were already his.

MATTHEY: What did the repeater mean to your uncle?

FÉLIX: Everything. Four years on one watch, and he meant it to strike the first minute of the new century. He would have wanted the whole town to hear it. I think he loved that piece more than anything in the house, and I think he loved it more than he loved the people in it. That is unkind of me. I have had a good deal of brandy.

MATTHEY: Thank you, Monsieur Monnier. Please remain in the house until I have spoken to the others.

FÉLIX: Of course. And if you are wondering, Inspector, I do not think you will want my watch. Nobody would give you a franc for it. Ask anyone.
