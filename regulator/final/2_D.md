*Interview with Dr Casimir Vauthier, physician, in the dining room. Second interview of the night.*

MATTHEY: Thank you for waiting, Doctor. You dined here tonight. Tell me about the evening, in your own order.

DR VAUTHIER: My own order is the only one I have left at this hour, Inspector. Aurèle gave his dinner as he has given it for thirty years. When the temple bell struck nine he made us all bring out our watches, and he went round the table like a jeweller at a fair, telling each of us what was wrong with ours. Mine was two minutes ahead of the bell. He said so with some satisfaction and told me to leave it alone, which I have never once managed to do.

MATTHEY: And after dinner?

DR VAUTHIER: Madame and I played piquet in the salon. She beats me more often than I care to admit, though I have the better of her on the cards with a Latin tag, which she finds unforgivable. *Tempus edax rerum*, Inspector. Piquet is the most honest way I know of losing an hour. We played until a quarter to eleven by my watch. Then I wanted my cigar case, which was in my coat in the hall, so I went out to fetch it. Madame began to play the piano as I went out. She played Chopin, as she does when she is thinking of something she will not say. She stopped when I came back.

MATTHEY: Was the cigar case where you had left it?

DR VAUTHIER: It was. I have no talent for hiding things, which is why I am a physician and not a policeman. I sat down again and lit a cigar, and the hall clock struck eleven a moment after I sat down again.

MATTHEY: You have seen Monnier's body.

DR VAUTHIER: I have. Rosa was in a terrible state, as you can imagine, and I took what I had in my bag, which was foolish, because nothing in it could help him. There was a blow to the back of the head, from behind, with something heavy. I could see at once that he had not suffered for long. Death would have been very quick. I will say that to Odile if she asks me, and I will say it kindly, though I doubt she will ask.

MATTHEY: And the regulator on the wall?

DR VAUTHIER: Stopped. I looked at it, I confess, because I could not look at him for long.

MATTHEY: You knew the family well before this evening.

DR VAUTHIER: I knew them all, and I knew Aurèle longest of anyone outside his own house. I attended Jules Monnier in his last illness, in 1889. Aurèle sat by that bed and did not once look at his watch, which for Aurèle was a sign of grief greater than any he ever showed to me. He was a difficult man to be fond of and an impossible one not to be fond of. Those are the two things I shall remember when the rest of this becomes a report.

MATTHEY: Did he speak to you tonight about his repeater?

DR VAUTHIER: He spoke of nothing else in the last years, and of nothing but its price in the last weeks. Girard wanted it and Aurèle would not sell it, and they quarrelled over it at table as men will quarrel over a thing they cannot have. I took no part. I am a doctor. I only hold the coats.

MATTHEY: Thank you, Doctor. I must ask you not to leave the town until we have finished.

DR VAUTHIER: I have no intention of leaving a game of piquet unfinished, Inspector. Madame will see to that, and she will see to it that I lose.
