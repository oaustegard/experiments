*Interview with Monsieur Bastien Girard, établisseur of Le Locle, in the dining room. Sixth interview of the night.*

MATTHEY: Monsieur Girard, you know why you are here. Aurèle Monnier was found dead in his atelier on the top floor of this house. I need to know what you did this evening and where you were. Tell it in your own order, and take your time.

GIRARD: I am no part of his household, Inspecteur. I buy movements from him now and then, and parts when I need them. He asked the trade men of the town to dine with the family on the last evening of the year, as he did every year. I came up from Le Locle for it. That is all my connection with the house.

MATTHEY: Tell me about the dinner.

GIRARD: The master made us show our watches and tell him what was wrong with them. He does it every year, and he enjoys it. I would not show him mine. A merchant's watch is his business and not a parlour game. He can say what he likes about my watch to my face, but he does not get to look at it first.

MATTHEY: Who was at the table?

GIRARD: Madame Monnier, the nephew Félix, Dr Vauthier, the girl from his atelier, and myself. I dislike the doctor's company, and I will say so plainly. He talks as if every room were a lecture hall and every guest a student who has not done his reading.

MATTHEY: Was there any disagreement at dinner?

GIRARD: There was. I had asked him for the rights to the repeater he has been building these four years. I offered him a fair price to sell it under my firm's name. He said it was not for sale at any price I could pay. We quarrelled about the figure across the table, and the quarrel did not end when the soup was taken away.

MATTHEY: And Monsieur Félix Monnier?

GIRARD: Félix owes me money. It is not a great sum, but he owes it, and I am not a man who hides his bills. I do not raise it at a dinner table, but I will not pretend I am sorry to be paid by a man who is about to inherit. You may ask him about it. He will tell you more than I would.

MATTHEY: After dinner, what did you do?

GIRARD: I sat on alone in the dining room with the brandy. I heard the temple strike ten there. I did not look at my watch.

MATTHEY: Why not?

GIRARD: Because I had no need of it. A man in my trade knows the hour without taking his watch from his waistcoat, and I was not waiting for anything.

MATTHEY: What happened next?

GIRARD: I went out into the street to smoke. I had one of my Brissago cigars, the cheap ones I buy by the box. It was twenty-one minutes past ten by my watch when I went out. It was a hard frost, with old snow in the street. I stayed under the front windows of the house. I like the cold when I want to think, and I stood there with a figure to think about.

MATTHEY: Did anything happen while you stood there?

GIRARD: The piano started up in the salon just as I lit my cigar. It was twenty-nine minutes past ten by my watch.

MATTHEY: Go on.

GIRARD: At twenty-six minutes to eleven by my watch I heard faint quick bells from the master's window up above.

MATTHEY: Quick bells. Describe them as you heard them, if you can.

GIRARD: Faint, and quick, and from the top of the house. That is all I have to say of them.

MATTHEY: And when did you come back into the house?

GIRARD: At fourteen minutes to eleven by my watch. The hall clock had just struck eleven, and its face showed two minutes past eleven. I went straight up to Félix in the library.

MATTHEY: Thank you, Monsieur Girard. Please do not leave the town until I have spoken to you again.

GIRARD: I have no wish to leave it, Inspecteur. It is far too cold to travel.
