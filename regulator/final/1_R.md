*Interview with Rosa Häfeli, maid and cook, in the dining room. First interview of the night.*

MATTHEY: Sit down, please, Madame Häfeli. You are not in any trouble. I only want to know about the house, the master, and this evening. Take your time.

ROSA: Ja, Monsieur. I have been in this house since I came down from the Emmental as a girl. I cook, I keep the house, I see to the linen and the silver. Monsieur Aurèle was hard to please about bread and harder about watches, but he paid his people, and he was never unkind to me.

MATTHEY: Tell me about him.

ROSA: He was always up in the atelier, under the long windows, from the morning. He came down only for his meals. He has been at one watch for a very long time, and he would not have anyone near his bench while he worked, only Fräulein Lina, because she was the only one who could do the work he wanted done.

MATTHEY: Let us come to this evening. What clock do you keep in the kitchen?

ROSA: My kitchen clock is a quarter of an hour fast. I keep it so on purpose, so nothing is ever late. The master teased me for it, but the bread was never late and neither was the dinner. Ask anyone in the house.

MATTHEY: Then tell me what happened by your clock.

ROSA: Fräulein Lina came down into the kitchen at thirteen minutes to eleven by my kitchen clock, and she stayed with me until after the temple struck eleven. She was very white. She sat by the stove and did not want to talk, which is not like her. She usually tells me about the movements the way other girls tell about their sweethearts.

MATTHEY: While she was with you, did you hear anything?

ROSA: We heard Madame's piano begin at two minutes to eleven by my kitchen clock, and stop at eleven minutes past eleven by my kitchen clock. Madame plays the Chopin, as she always does, and she plays it very well. I know the sound of that piano through two floors and a stove.

MATTHEY: Did you hear anything else?

ROSA: I heard the hall clock strike eleven when my kitchen clock showed thirteen minutes past eleven. I looked at my clock, as I always do when the hall strikes. That is all I heard that I can tell you.

MATTHEY: Let us go on to the toast. What was happening in the kitchen then?

ROSA: The champagne glasses were ready on the tray. I had washed them myself that morning and polished them twice, because Madame wanted them for the new year. When it was time for the toast, I went up the stairs to fetch the master.

MATTHEY: Tell me what you found.

ROSA: He was on the floor of the atelier, by his bench. There was blood on the boards. The brass mallet from his bench was on the floor beside him. The regulator on the wall had stopped. His little watch, the one that strikes when you press the slide, was in his hand. Herrgott, Monsieur, I did not touch him. I went down the stairs and I could not speak for a while.

MATTHEY: You are frightened of me, I think.

ROSA: Ja, Monsieur. I have never been asked anything by the police. My papers are in order and I have done nothing wrong. But I do not want to be put in a cell for a thing I did not do, and I do not want Madame made to suffer more than she must. She is a proud woman. She will not cry in front of you, so do not expect it.

MATTHEY: Thank you, Rosa. Is there anything you would like to add?

ROSA: Only that the kitchen is clean and the glasses are ready. Whatever you need, Monsieur, you will find me there.
