# The Regulator — bible

## Setting

La Chaux-de-Fonds, canton of Neuchâtel, in the Jura at a thousand metres. The town is
a grid of long stone houses built for watchmaking, each top floor lit by a row of long
windows so the benches get the daylight. Night of Sunday 31 December 1899. Hard frost,
old snow in the street, the town quiet before midnight. The Grand Temple bell strikes
the true hours over the roofs.

The house of Aurèle Monnier, master watchmaker, Rue du Progrès. Top floor: the atelier,
his bench under the long windows, a stove, the precision regulator on the wall. First
floor: the library, directly under the atelier; its ceiling is the atelier's pine floor.
Ground floor: the hall with the stairs and an old Morbier long-case clock that strikes
the hours by its own face and runs fast; the salon at the front, with the piano and a
marble mantel clock; the dining room; the kitchen at the back with Rosa's cheap
American alarm clock.

Aurèle Monnier, 62, spent four years on one watch: a minute repeater he meant to strike
the first minute of the new century at midnight. A minute repeater sounds the time when
you press its slide: low notes for the hours, a doubled note for each quarter, high notes
for the minutes past the quarter.

At dinner, when the temple bell struck nine, Monnier made his guests show their
watches, as he did every year, and told each owner what was wrong with theirs.

At ten to midnight Rosa went up to fetch him for the toast and found him dead on the
atelier floor, struck from behind with the brass mallet from his own bench. The
regulator had stopped. His repeater was in his hand.

## The inspector

Inspecteur Léon Matthey of the cantonal Sûreté, 40s, arrived from the police post at
twenty to one in the morning. He interviews each person alone in the dining room
between one and four. Courteous and patient, he asks short questions and lets people
talk. He never tells one witness what another said.

## The six

**Odile Monnier**, 55, the wife. A Neuchâtel pastor's daughter. Composed, exact, dry
and formal: short sentences, the occasional cold irony. She plays the piano well,
Chopin especially. After thirty years of marriage her love for her husband was worn
smooth. She does not cry in front of the inspector. She is fond of the doctor, the
family's friend for thirty years.

**Félix Monnier**, 28, the nephew and heir. Son of Aurèle's younger brother Jules, who
died in 1889. Charming and frightened. He talks too much and jokes when he is afraid.
He owes money, some of it to Bastien Girard. He carries his late father's silver watch,
which has run badly for ten years; he has never let anyone touch it, his uncle included.
His uncle mocked it at dinner. He drank brandy alone in the library after dinner.

**Lina Ruedi**, 24, the régleuse: she adjusts hairsprings and regulates watches, and she
is the best hand in the atelier. A farmer's daughter from Le Locle. She speaks in
numbers and exact observations and keeps her feelings out of her voice. She was
devoted to the master's work. She worked beside him in the atelier until he sent her
downstairs.

**Dr Casimir Vauthier**, 58, physician, the family's doctor and friend for thirty years.
Urbane and warm. Fond of cigars, of piquet and of Latin tags. He is very helpful to the
inspector and offers medical observations, but he gives the inspector no hour for the
death beyond what is in his statement. He attended Jules Monnier in his last illness in
1889.

**Bastien Girard**, 45, établisseur from Le Locle: he buys movements and parts and
assembles and sells watches under his firm's name. Blunt and commercial, a big man.
He smokes cheap Brissago cigars. He wanted to buy the rights to the repeater, and he and
Monnier quarrelled about the price at dinner. He dislikes the doctor's company. He
walks in the cold to think.

**Rosa Häfeli**, 40, maid and cook, from the Emmental. Her French is plain, with a
German word now and then ("Herrgott", "Fräulein", "ja"). She keeps her kitchen clock
fast on purpose so that nothing is ever late. She is loyal, frightened of the police and
proud of her kitchen. She found the body.

## Register

Period-plausible, without pastiche. The voice of an interview transcript as a careful
clerk would take it down: the witness's own words, lightly tidied. Characters say what
they saw and heard, with the feelings of people woken into a death at the turn of the
century. Nobody is a detective, and nobody does arithmetic about clocks for the reader.
