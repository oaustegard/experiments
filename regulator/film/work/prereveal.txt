INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): At ten minutes to midnight, on the last night of the nineteenth century, a maid named Rosa Häfeli climbed the stairs of a watchmaker's house in La Chaux-de-Fonds with a tray of champagne glasses.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): She was going to fetch her master for the toast.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): Aurèle Monnier lay on the floor of his atelier. Someone had struck him once, from behind, with the brass mallet from his own bench.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): On the wall above him, the regulator had stopped. Its hands stood at five past ten.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): And in his right hand was the watch he had spent four years making. A minute repeater, with its slide pressed home.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): My name is Léon Matthey. In the winter of 1899 I was an inspector of the cantonal police, and I was called to that house a little after the bells had rung in the new century.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): La Chaux-de-Fonds is a town built for clocks. After the great fire they laid its streets out in a grid, and every house has a top floor of long windows, so that the benches catch the daylight.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): Monnier worked at the top of his house, under those windows, beside his best hand, a young régleuse named Lina Ruedi.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): A minute repeater sounds the time when you press its slide. One low note for each hour. A doubled note for each quarter past the hour. And a high note for each minute past the quarter.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): Monnier pressed it as he fell. Whoever struck him was standing in that room when it sounded.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): Six people were in the house that night. His wife, Odile, a pastor's daughter, who played Chopin and did not weep.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): His nephew Félix, the heir, who owed money to half the town.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): Lina Ruedi, who regulated watches for a living and spoke in measurements.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): Doctor Casimir Vauthier, physician to the family and its friend for thirty years.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): Bastien Girard, a watch merchant from Le Locle, who wanted to buy the repeater and had quarrelled with Monnier over its price.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): And Rosa, the maid, who kept her kitchen clock fast so that nothing would ever be late.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): Every year at dinner, when the temple bell struck nine, Monnier made his guests show him their watches, and told each of them what was wrong with it.
ODILE MONNIER: Félix's watch showed twenty minutes to nine. Aurèle scolded him for it.
LINA RUEDI: Mine was exact. The doctor's was two minutes ahead. Monsieur Girard would not take his out of his pocket.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): Remember those watches. Every clock in that house kept its own time, and before morning I would need all of them.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): I took them one at a time, in the dining room, between one and four in the morning. I spoke to Rosa first. She had found him.
ROSA HÄFELI: My kitchen clock is a quarter of an hour fast. I keep it so on purpose, so nothing is ever late.
ROSA HÄFELI: Fräulein Lina came down into the kitchen at thirteen minutes to eleven by my kitchen clock, and she stayed with me until after the temple struck eleven.
ROSA HÄFELI: We heard Madame's piano begin at two minutes to eleven by my kitchen clock, and stop at eleven minutes past eleven.
ROSA HÄFELI: I heard the hall clock strike eleven when my kitchen clock showed thirteen minutes past eleven.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): The doctor had examined the body. He was very helpful, as doctors are.
DR VAUTHIER: Madame and I played piquet in the salon until a quarter to eleven by my watch, when I went out to the hall to fetch my cigar case from my coat. Madame began to play the piano as I went out, and she stopped when I came back.
DR VAUTHIER: The hall clock struck eleven a moment after I sat down again. Tempus edax rerum, Inspector. Time devours all things, the clocks included.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): Madame Monnier received me as if I had come about the gas.
ODILE MONNIER: The doctor and I played piquet without a break. The temple struck ten while we played. We played on until he went out to fetch his cigars. I did not look at the clock when he went.
ODILE MONNIER: As he went out he asked me to play for him. I sat down at the piano at once and played until he came back, and I stopped when he came in.
ODILE MONNIER: A moment after he came back, the hall clock struck eleven.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): Lina Ruedi answered every question as if it were a measurement.
LINA RUEDI: After dinner I worked beside the master in the atelier. The hall clock downstairs struck ten when the regulator showed twelve minutes to ten. When the temple struck ten, the regulator agreed with the bell exactly.
LINA RUEDI: He sent me downstairs when the regulator showed twenty past ten. It was running then, and he was well. I went straight down to the kitchen.
LINA RUEDI: In the kitchen I looked at Rosa's clock when Madame's piano began. It showed two minutes to eleven. When the piano stopped, it showed eleven minutes past eleven.
LINA RUEDI: I stayed in the kitchen with Rosa until after the temple struck eleven.
LINA RUEDI: That afternoon I saw the master in the kitchen with Rosa's clock in his hands, laughing. I did not ask why.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): Félix Monnier had spent the evening alone in the library, directly beneath his uncle's bench. He knew how that looked.
FÉLIX MONNIER: At a quarter past ten by my watch, someone went quickly up the stairs past the library door. I did not see who. The door was shut.
FÉLIX MONNIER: At eighteen minutes past ten by my watch I heard a thump on the floor right above me. Then my uncle's repeater. Ten slow deep notes, I counted those. Then some doubled notes, then quick high ones. I did not count those.
FÉLIX MONNIER: At twenty-one minutes past ten by my watch, someone came quickly down. Again I did not see who.
FÉLIX MONNIER: Bastien Girard came into the library at thirty-two minutes past ten by my watch, and we sat together there until the temple struck eleven.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): Bastien Girard had spent the worst of it out in the frost.
BASTIEN GIRARD: I went out into the street to smoke at twenty-one minutes past ten by my watch, and I stayed under the front windows of the house.
BASTIEN GIRARD: The piano started up in the salon just as I lit my cigar, at twenty-nine minutes past ten by my watch.
BASTIEN GIRARD: At twenty-six minutes to eleven by my watch I heard faint quick bells from the master's window up above.
BASTIEN GIRARD: I came back into the house at fourteen minutes to eleven by my watch. The hall clock had just struck eleven, and its face showed two minutes past eleven. I went straight up to Félix in the library.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): That is everything I was told. One of the six lied to me. The other five told me honestly what they saw and heard, though what any of them believed about a clock was another matter.
INSPECTOR MATTHEY (narrating): No clock was touched that evening except by the murderer's hand. The temple bell keeps true time. And no one in that house needs more than five minutes to go from any room to any other.
