*Interview with Madame Odile Monnier, in the dining room. Third interview of the night.*

MATTHEY: Madame, thank you for waiting. Please sit down. I know you have had a dreadful shock. Tell me about the evening in your own way.

MME MONNIER: There is no evening in my own way, Inspector. There is only the evening that happened. We dined as we do every year. My husband liked to have everyone show their watches when the temple struck nine. He did it at the table, with his napkin on his knee, like a magistrate. When the bell struck he held out his hand and told each of us what was wrong with our watch.

MATTHEY: Do you remember what he said to Félix?

MME MONNIER: Félix's watch showed twenty minutes to nine. Aurèle scolded him for it in front of everyone. Félix laughed, as he does when he is embarrassed, and said that his father's silver watch had run badly for ten years and that he would not let anyone touch it, not even his uncle. My husband said it was the only honest thing Félix had said all evening. Félix has always had a great deal of charm and very little money. I do not think my husband ever forgave him for either.

MATTHEY: Was there any other disagreement at dinner?

MME MONNIER: Monsieur Girard and my husband quarrelled about the repeater. Monsieur Girard wished to buy the rights to it, and he thought the price was unreasonable. My husband thought the price was exactly what the watch was worth after four years of work. They raised their voices, and then they lowered them, which was worse. I do not think either of them enjoyed the rest of the meal.

MATTHEY: Later, in the salon. Tell me what you did there.

MME MONNIER: Casimir and I played piquet. We have played piquet on Sunday evenings for thirty years. He is the oldest friend we have, Inspector. He was at our wedding and he attended Jules when Jules was dying, and he has never once in all that time let a card fall from his hand without a remark about it. We played without a break. The temple struck ten while we played, and we did not stop.

MATTHEY: And then the doctor left the room.

MME MONNIER: He went out to fetch his cigars. He had left them in his coat. He went at twenty-eight minutes past ten by the salon clock. As he went, he asked me to play for him. He is fond of my playing, and he says it is the only thing in the house that does not need winding. I sat down at the piano at once. I played Chopin. I shall not tell you which piece, because you will write it down and it will mean nothing to you. I played until he came back, and I stopped when he came in.

MATTHEY: Did he say anything when he came back?

MME MONNIER: He said that I had played badly, which was a lie, and that his cigars were damp, which was true. A moment after he came back the hall clock struck eleven.

MATTHEY: You have not asked me what has happened to your husband.

MME MONNIER: You will tell me when you wish to. I have been asked the same questions three times tonight, by people who were frightened, and I have answered them. I shall not weep for you, Inspector, if that is what you are waiting for. I was married to Aurèle for thirty years. Our affection had been worn smooth long before this evening, the way a good stone is worn on a step. I do not expect you to understand that, and I do not require you to.

MATTHEY: Thank you, Madame. That is all for now.

MME MONNIER: Then I should like to go up to my room. I should like to be alone, if you permit it.
