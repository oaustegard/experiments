*Interview with Dr Casimir Vauthier, physician, in the dining room. Second interview of the night.*

DR VAUTHIER: Forgive me, Inspector. I have sat in this chair and I still cannot make my hands believe what Rosa told us. Aurèle Monnier. I have been his friend for thirty years. I attended his brother Jules in his last illness, in 1889, so I have sat in this house through a death before. It does not get easier.

MATTHEY: I am sorry for your loss, Doctor. I will keep you only as long as I must. You dined here with the family?

DR VAUTHIER: As I do every year on the last day of the year. When the temple struck nine, Aurèle had us all show him our watches. It is his custom. He tells each man what is wrong with his watch, and he is seldom wrong. Mine was two minutes ahead of the bell. He had something to say about it, as he always does.

MATTHEY: And afterwards?

DR VAUTHIER: Madame and I sat down to piquet in the salon. Odile plays a better hand than most men I know, and a far better piano. We played until a quarter to eleven by my watch, when I went out to the hall to fetch my cigar case from my coat. Madame began to play the piano as I went out, and she stopped when I came back.

MATTHEY: Go on with the game, Doctor. Tell me what else you remember of it.

DR VAUTHIER: When the temple struck ten during our game, I noticed the salon clock showed five minutes to ten, and I remarked on it to Madame. In that house one notices clocks, Inspector. There are clocks in every room of it.

MATTHEY: Thank you. Now I must ask about the atelier. What did you learn of Aurèle's death?

DR VAUTHIER: A blow to the back of the head, from behind. I am not a surgeon, but I have seen enough wounds to say that death would have been very quick. I would like to think he did not know much of it.

MATTHEY: Can you tell me the time of death?

DR VAUTHIER: No, and I would be a fool to try. I have told you what I can say as a physician. *Tempus edax rerum*, Inspector. Time devours all things, the clocks included. I will not give you a time I cannot prove. You have men trained for that.

MATTHEY: And the regulator in the atelier?

DR VAUTHIER: It had stopped. That is all I know of it. Aurèle loved that regulator more than most of his friends.

MATTHEY: What is Madame Monnier like, in grief?

DR VAUTHIER: Composed. She will not weep in front of you, and I would not ask her to. She has been a pastor's daughter all her life, and she keeps her feelings where she keeps her good linen. I have a great respect for her.

MATTHEY: Thank you, Doctor. You may smoke, if you wish.

DR VAUTHIER: I thank you. I have my cigar case, which I fetched myself.
