Monsieur l'Inspecteur,

By the time you read this I shall be out in the frost, and you will know why I did not wait for you. I will not repeat what is already in your notes. I will tell you what I did not say in the dining room, and what the piano had to do with it.

Odile and I had been at piquet in the salon since the table was cleared. When I rose to fetch my cigars I asked her to play for me. She played the second of the Op. 9 nocturnes, which she plays better than anyone I have heard, and she did not ask why I wanted it. I had chosen the music for its length and its volume. The piano would cover my steps on the stairs.

Aurèle had told me in the hall, before dinner, that he would tell Félix at midnight and the authorities after. "At the turn of the century I settle my accounts," he said, and went in to be looked at by his guests. He had found his brother's last letter among the papers. It described the doses. I had written them in my own hand, and I had signed the certificate beneath them.

I went up past the library door, which was shut, and I made no sound on the stairs that the piano did not cover. Aurèle was at his bench under the long windows with the repeater in his hand. He did not turn; he never turned from the bench for anyone. I struck him once from behind with the mallet from his own bench. He fell, and his hand closed on the slide, and the repeater sounded. You have had it described to you: the hours low, the quarter doubled, the minutes high. I counted it as anyone in this town would count it. It said ten thirty-eight.

I stood there some moments. Then I did the only thing a physician thinks of with a clock. I stopped the pendulum and moved the hands back to five past ten, an hour when I had sat at cards with Odile in plain view of the whole salon.

I came down, took the cigar case from my coat in the hall, and went back into the salon. Odile stopped playing when I came in. She did not look up. In thirty years she has never once asked me where I have been, and she did not ask then. The hall clock struck eleven a moment after I sat down.

When you questioned me I told you I had gone out at a quarter to eleven by my watch, after the repeater would have sounded. That was false in every part. My watch runs two minutes fast. I have never corrected it, from vanity, and you will find it so in my waistcoat. The rest I told you truly.

I ask you to see that Odile is not made to answer the newspapers. She has done nothing but play the piano in her own house. Rosa is to have her wages through the end of the month, and I should be obliged if Félix were told that his uncle meant to speak to him at midnight, and that it was not Félix's fault that he did not.

I have no more to add.

Casimir Vauthier
